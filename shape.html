Distribution shape

Why one aorta can have very different z-scores

Every z-score assumes a shape for the healthy distribution. Near the middle the shape barely matters; for a severely dilated aorta it can double the z-score. Move the slider to see how.

What λ does

A z-score needs three things: the expected (median) diameter, the spread, and the shape of the distribution. The shape is set by the Box-Cox power, λ. With λ = 0, diameters are log-normal, so being 20% above the median counts the same at any size. With λ = 1 they are normal in millimetres, which is the assumption behind ratio z-scores such as diameter ÷ BSA0.5. With λ = 2 they are normal on the scale of an area, as with CSA/height.

The calculator estimates λ from the healthy children for each level instead of assuming it. Here the median and spread stay at the fitted values for the child below, and only λ changes.

What the data say

The fitted λ is about 0.05 at the sinuses (essentially log-normal), and about 0.4 at the sinotubular junction and the ascending aorta. A shape of 2 was strongly rejected at every level. Because the median and spread here stay fixed while λ moves, the alternative shapes are illustrations rather than refitted models; a full refit at a fixed λ also adjusts the spread slightly.

No healthy child in the reference data sits much beyond z ±4, so past that point any z-score, from any calculator, depends on the assumed shape rather than on data. That is why the calculator also reports percent of expected.
